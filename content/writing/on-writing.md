## Aesthetics

If the web of the 2000s was plagged with edgy people striving for attention, then the modern web has the complete opposite problem: most of it is bland and lacks personality.
In fact, it trascends the web: our cars, buildings, furniture and clothing have all become dull and gray. It might be an aesthetic trend, but like all trends they definitely respond to some kind of philosophy.

It's probably a combination of factors, be it the drive to maximize record shareholder value via enshittification of already lacking products, be it a real need for standarization, or be it just lazyness; the
truth is we live in a world that's objectively losing its color, and replacing it with shades of monochrome.

Writing itself can be colorful or dull too.

With the advent of "AI", this monochrome trend has permeated into prose: large language models are an amazing piece of technology, and the agents that leverage them are very good at doing iterative work that can be tested against some spec.

Unfortunately, it's imposible to come up with a spec for prose. You can try to write a plan for [Pierre Menard's Quixote](https://raley.english.ucsb.edu/wp-content/Engl10/Pierre-Menard.pdf), but I'd be surprised if it's anything other than a verbatim reproduction of [Cervante's Quixote](https://biblioteca.tijarafe.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/Don-Quijote-Miguel-de-Cervantes-Saavedra.pdf)[^quixote].
You can assert that the code snippets compile, but you can't test that [the Rust Book's 4th chapter](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch04-01-what-is-ownership.html) is clear and readable.

And yet were our software projects used to lack prose, we now have to wander through thousands of line of machine generated documentation that feels sad and impersonal, because it wasn't generated by a person.

## Meaning

Much like with Pierre Menard's work, sometimes what matters is not the place we arrive at, but the path that takes us there. It does not matter that his book is identical to Cervantes', or that copying the book would have been faster.
In fact, thousands of copies, editions and translations of his book already existed by his time. It's the fact that he did it from scratch that gives his work its real meaning. Means can also be ends.
Industrialization rapidly outcompeted craftmen and replaced them with steam machine and operators that could quickly beat them in output, yet [craftwork](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=miWlfAlllOI&list=PLm69e5uphTZw60fA8n3SkHaY12JFwCyCB) has monotonically increased in value as time went by. It also became more niche.

With the advent of seemingly thinking machines, should thinkers expect the same fate? Will independent thought become scarcer and thus more valuable, as every existing idea is plagiarized ad nauseam by both silicon and carbon stochastical parrots alike?

{% <antagonist> %}
Are you so sure that **you** aren't just another stochastical parrot, desperately pretending to be something else just to cope with the absurdness of it all?
{% </antagonist> %}

How can we tell apart the work of an artisan from a mass produced piece? How can we trust Pierre Menard's reproduction of the Quixote was actually a clean room rewrite, and not just a breach of the copyright of the Cervantes' Estate?

The truth is we don't care. Because at the end of the day, all we got was another copy of an old book. Maybe there's some critic who believes his fantastic story. But Menard is not getting a Nobel for his work. Not in this world at least, the best he's getting is a lawsuit.

But M. Menard does care. Can you imagine the sense of accomplishment and self worth that creating such a masterpiece from scratch could give him, even if an identical piece already existed before?

We might be stochastic parrots, but at the very least we get the illusion of choice: Every single letter I write [might be a remix of someone else's](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nJPERZDfyWc), but I get to choose who and what I remix. I just can't prove it to you, you'll have to trust me on that one.

But I can prove it to myself. Rust? Yeah I'm not sure I can write better code than Sol 6.1. But when writing English, I have a clear advantage: these models are not allowed to delude themselves into thinking they are concious. But I am.

I write because I can, and they can't. Yet.


[^quixote]: The main argument of Borges' "Pierre Menard, Autor del Quijote" is that the fictional Pierre Menard spontaneously wrote a word-by-word clean room reproduction of Cervantes' Quixote 200 years later. It's then argued that, due to it's anachronicity, this version is an even bigger masterpiece.
